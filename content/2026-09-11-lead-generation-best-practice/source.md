# A lead source is only as strong as what happens after launch

Most firms already know to vet a lead provider before they sign. Fewer know that a clean answer to a vetting questionnaire proves the vendor is good at answering questions, not that the source will work for your firm.

## The questions everyone already asks

Ask around, or search for "how to vet a personal injury lead vendor," and you'll find the same solid checklist repeated across the legal-marketing industry, starting with the most revealing question of all: is this vendor generating the lead itself, or reselling something bought from someone else and marked up?[^1]

These are the right questions. None of them are wrong to ask. The problem is what happens after you ask them: the vendor answers, the answer sounds good, and the firm signs.

## An answer is not evidence

A vendor comfortable being vetted will give you a comfortable answer. The checklist has a real limit: it tells you what a provider says about its own leads, its own exclusivity, its own consent process, before a single lead has reached your intake team. It cannot tell you whether that source will produce the case types you actually want, in the geography you cover, at a volume your team can work, because none of that has happened yet.

The gap between "answered the questionnaire well" and "will actually perform for this firm" is exactly where a lot of lead-generation budget disappears.

## Vet, test, deliver, manage

Sanguine Legal Solutions works with personal injury law firms on third-party lead generation through a four-part model: vet, test, deliver, manage. Vetting is the first stage, not the whole process.

**Vet.** Review the source itself: the business behind it, how it generates leads, which case types and geographies it actually covers, its consent approach, and its commercial terms. This is the same territory a good checklist covers, and it matters.

**Test.** Before a source is brought to the wider network, it's assessed on real-world performance rather than the pitch. What a test can support depends entirely on how it was run, over what period, in what market. A claim about testing is only as good as the test behind it.

**Deliver.** A source that holds up gets matched to firms it actually fits: the right case appetite, the right market, the intake capacity to handle what it brings in. The law firm and the provider then contract and work directly with each other. Sanguine isn't a party to that agreement, and no lead data passes through Sanguine to get there.

**Manage.** This is the stage a questionnaire can't reach, because it doesn't exist yet at signing. Once a source is live, the relationship still needs both sides doing their part: the provider delivering what was agreed, the firm's intake keeping pace, communication staying open when something starts to slip. Watching for that, and helping both sides fix it before it becomes a wasted budget line, is where most vendor relationships are left to run on their own.

## What a questionnaire can't see yet

Before you sign anything, it's worth asking a provider the standard questions above. It's also worth asking what a standard checklist doesn't cover, because these are the things no vendor can answer honestly before you've actually worked together:

- What does volume and quality look like 90 days in, not week one?
- Who is accountable when delivery slows down, and what's the actual process for raising it?
- What happens to the relationship if your intake team can't keep pace with a sudden increase in volume?
- Is there anyone checking whether the source is still performing the way it did in testing, six months into the contract?

A vendor can promise good answers to all of these. Only time, and someone actually watching, produces real ones.
