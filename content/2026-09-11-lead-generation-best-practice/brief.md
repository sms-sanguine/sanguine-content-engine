# Brief: Lead-Generation Best Practice (Sanguine Legal Solutions)

**Status: APPROVED** — approved by s.foley@sanguinesa.com in chat, 2026-09-11.

Brand pack used: `brand-pack-snapshot.md` in this folder (fetched live from Notion, 2026-09-11T03:12:53.179Z). All later steps read that snapshot, not Notion.

## Pillar
**Vet the source** (primary), with a secondary line into **Test the promise** — the piece should make clear that vetting on paper and testing in the field are two different, sequential steps, and a checklist alone only covers the first.

## Reader
Managing partner or firm owner at a U.S. personal injury firm (primary), read secondarily by the firm's CMO/marketing leader. The trigger: they are either about to sign with a new third-party lead provider, or they've been burned by one, and they are searching for how to evaluate a vendor before they commit budget — not for general PI marketing advice.

## The single idea
A vetting checklist only tells you what a provider says about itself before the contract starts. The questions that actually predict whether a lead source will work for your firm can't be answered until someone tests it against real intake, in your case types and geography, and keeps watching after launch.

## Why us
Every competitor piece we found is a static checklist: a list of questions to ask a vendor, written once, answered once, then filed away. Sanguine can say something none of them can, because it's true of Sanguine's own approved operating model and no one else's: vetting is stage one of a four-stage process — vet, test, deliver, manage — where a source is tested before it's introduced more broadly, and where Sanguine stays involved after the firm signs to catch the problems a pre-contract questionnaire can't surface (intake readiness, delivery consistency, whether the firm and provider are actually operating the way they agreed to). The checklist bloggers are telling firms how to interrogate a vendor once. Sanguine's story is that vetting alone was never going to be enough, and explains why.

## Proof to use
Only from the snapshot's "Approved strategic proof" list:
- No leads change hands through Sanguine — a firm-first fact worth stating plainly to draw the "we're not a lead seller" line early.
- Firm-first assessment — Sanguine looks at case-type appetite, geography, volume and intake capacity before any provider conversation happens.
- Third-party provider vetting — described as vetting, never as certification or a guarantee.
- Sources are tested before broader delivery — the vet → test sequence, tied to the actual **Vet. Test. Deliver. Manage.** model language and order.
- Ongoing management — the piece should end here: vetting doesn't stop when the contract is signed.
- Direct commercial relationship — the firm and provider contract directly; Sanguine is not a party to that agreement.
- Free, no-obligation lead-generation review — the CTA-adjacent offer, confirm it's still current before the draft ships.

Do not use any figure, count, rate, named client, named provider or testimonial — all are flagged **Unknown** / not yet approved in the snapshot.

## Compliance flags
- Any description of the vetting or testing process must stay descriptive ("reviews," "assesses," "tests real-world performance") — never "certifies," "approves as compliant," or "guarantees."
- Do not let the "test" section imply a specific test proves future performance for any given firm — the snapshot requires tying testing claims to actual method/evidence, not implying a general guarantee.
- No provider comparison, ranking, or named example — even hypothetical ones must stay generic enough not to read as a real vendor.
- If the piece touches consent, TCPA, or compliance obligations for the *reader's* vendor relationships, keep it general and point back to the firm's own legal review — Sanguine is not a law firm and doesn't give compliance sign-off.
- The "free, no-obligation review" CTA needs a check that the offer is still current before publishing (per snapshot note).
- Needs the required baseline disclosure that Sanguine doesn't sell leads / isn't a party to the firm-provider contract, stated in the body, not only a footer line.
- If any commission/compensation angle comes up in framing why this process is trustworthy, flag for review — the compensation model and disclosure requirements are still **Unknown** in the snapshot.

## What this is NOT
- Not another "23 lead-gen strategies" or "best personal injury lead companies in 2026" listicle — that ground is already crowded and it's not the story only Sanguine can tell.
- Not a ranking or comparison of named lead-generation vendors.
- Not a claim that Sanguine's process guarantees better leads, lower cost per case, or higher conversion — no performance promise, explicit or implied.
- Not general personal-injury marketing advice (SEO tips, ad spend, website conversion) — that's a different pillar and a different reader problem.
